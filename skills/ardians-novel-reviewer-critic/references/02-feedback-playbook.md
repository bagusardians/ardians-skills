# Feedback Playbook

How to turn a score into feedback the author can actually implement. Vague praise and
vague criticism are both failures. This file defines the feedback unit, the diagnosis
patterns per category, and worked examples.

## The feedback unit

Every item of feedback is one unit with five fields. Never write an item that is missing
a field, except `Evidence` when the item is a global pattern and you cite multiple
places.

1. **Location:** where it occurs (chapter, scene, page, paragraph, or a short quote).
2. **Observation:** what is on the page, stated neutrally and specifically.
3. **Diagnosis:** why it is a problem, tied to a named craft principle and to the reader
   effect it causes.
4. **Fix:** a concrete, implementable change. Describe the pattern to apply, then give a
   short example revision (a line or a sentence or two), not a full rewrite.
5. **Severity:** blocker, major, moderate, or minor.

Optionally add **Why it matters to this book** when the fix interacts with theme, arc, or
genre promise.

## Rules for implementable fixes

- Be specific enough that two different readers would make the same change. "Tighten the
  prose" is not a fix. "Cut the three sentences that restate the same feeling; keep the
  one concrete image" is.
- Give the smallest sufficient example. One revised line demonstrates the pattern.
- Name the technique, not just the symptom: "bury the lede," "scene vs summary," "filter
  words," "motivation-reaction unit," "try/fail cycle," "sentence rhythm."
- Preserve the author's voice. The example fix shows the move, it does not replace their
  style.
- Tie the fix to reader effect: what will the reader feel, understand, or anticipate
  differently after the change.
- Offer options when the correct fix is a genuine craft choice, and say what each option
  costs.
- For structural problems, give an order of operations, not just a diagnosis.

## Diagnosis patterns by category

Each pattern is a common failure with its signature, the principle it violates, and the
move that repairs it.

### Concept and originality

- **Default premise:** the setup is the genre's default with no angle. Principle: the
  promise must be distinctive. Fix: name the one twist or constraint only this book has
  and foreground it in the first act.
- **Unearned high concept:** a big idea that the story never uses. Principle: premise is a
  debt the plot must pay. Fix: list three plot turns that only this premise makes
  possible; cut premise elements that generate none.

### Story and plot

- **Episodic drift:** events happen to the character without causality. Principle: plot is
  cause and effect. Fix: for each scene, write "because of X, therefore Y"; scenes that
  fail the test get cut, merged, or reordered.
- **Weak stakes:** failure costs nothing. Principle: stakes must be concrete and personal.
  Fix: name what the protagonist loses if they fail, in a specific, tangible term.
- **Unearned resolution:** the ending arrives from outside the protagonist's actions.
  Principle: the protagonist must cause the climax. Fix: return the final decision to the
  protagonist and remove the rescue.
- **Promise/payoff gap:** setups that never pay or payoffs never set up. Principle of
  Chekhov's gun. Fix: build a setup/payoff ledger; add setups for orphan payoffs and pay
  or cut orphan setups.

### Structure and pacing

- **Sagging middle:** the middle repeats the same beat at the same intensity. Principle:
  escalation and try/fail cycles. Fix: map the middle into rising attempts, each failing
  worse, and add a midpoint reversal.
- **Late inciting incident:** the story starts before the story starts. Principle: start
  in motion. Fix: cut or compress the pre-story chapters; open on the disturbance.
- **Scene without a turn:** a scene that begins and ends in the same state. Principle:
  every scene changes something. Fix: give each scene an entry state and exit state; if
  they match, cut or merge.
- **Summary where scene is needed:** the most important moments are told, not shown.
  Principle: dramatize turning points. Fix: convert the summary to scene at the point of
  highest tension.

### Character and arc

- **Passive protagonist:** things happen to them; they decide nothing. Principle:
  character is revealed through choice under pressure. Fix: at each turn, ask what the
  protagonist chooses; if the answer is "nothing," rewrite the turn.
- **Flat arc:** the character ends where they began. Principle: want vs need and
  transformation. Fix: define the lie the character believes, the truth they must accept,
  and the moment they choose it.
- **Inconsistent motivation:** behavior contradicts established desire. Principle:
  motivation drives action. Fix: either change the action or add the scene that explains
  the shift.
- **Thin secondary cast:** side characters exist only to serve. Principle: everyone wants
  something. Fix: give each named character a want that sometimes conflicts with the
  protagonist's.

### Prose and voice

- **Filter words:** "she saw," "he felt," "she noticed." Principle: close point of view.
  Fix: delete the filter and render the perception directly.
- **Excessive telling of emotion:** naming the feeling instead of causing it. Principle:
  evoke, don't label. Fix: replace the emotion word with a specific action, image, or
  physical detail.
- **Monotone rhythm:** uniform sentence length and structure. Principle: rhythm carries
  tension. Fix: alternate long and short; end scenes on a short, hard sentence.
- **Purple prose:** ornament that obscures action. Principle: clarity first. Fix: cut one
  modifier per noun and one image per sentence; keep the strongest.
- **Head-hopping:** point of view jumps mid-scene. Principle: one POV per scene. Fix:
  assign a single viewpoint and recast the interior access of others as observable
  behavior.

### Worldbuilding and setting

- **Info dump:** rules delivered in a block. Principle: reveal through action. Fix: drip
  the rules across three scenes where they create a problem.
- **Inconsistent rules:** the setting's logic breaks when convenient. Principle: internal
  consistency. Fix: write the rules down; when a convenience violates one, change the
  scene or pay the cost of breaking it.
- **Wallpaper setting:** place names with no sensory or social texture. Principle:
  specificity creates place. Fix: add one concrete, sense-bound detail per scene that
  only this place would have.

### Theme and meaning

- **Thesis statement:** the theme is announced in dialogue or narration. Principle:
  dramatize, don't state. Fix: cut the statement and build a scene whose outcome argues it.
- **Unintegrated theme:** theme and plot run on separate tracks. Principle: theme emerges
  from the protagonist's arc. Fix: make the climactic choice embody the theme's answer.

### Dialogue

- **On-the-nose dialogue:** characters say exactly what they mean and feel. Principle:
  subtext. Fix: give each speaker a hidden want in the scene; let them pursue it around
  the stated topic.
- **Interchangeable voices:** everyone sounds the same. Principle: voice reflects
  identity. Fix: assign each speaker a diction, rhythm, and verbal habit; revise lines
  that ignore them.
- **Talking heads:** dialogue with no business or space. Principle: dialogue happens in a
  body, in a place. Fix: add specific physical action and setting pressure to the exchange.
- **As-you-know-Bob exposition:** characters explain what they both know. Principle:
  exposition must have an in-world reason. Fix: give the listener a reason not to know, or
  make the information contested.

### Emotional impact

- **Unearned emotion:** the reader is told to feel without the setup. Principle: emotion
  is the payoff of preparation. Fix: plant the attachment earlier; then let the blow land.
- **Flat tension:** no scene threatens what the reader values. Principle: tension needs
  jeopardy to a valued thing. Fix: state what is at risk in each act and raise it.
- **Unmemorable:** nothing lingers. Principle: a defining image or choice. Fix: give the
  book one unforgettable scene and protect it.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: pacing (structure and pacing, major)

- **Location:** Ch. 9-12, the pursuit through the market.
- **Observation:** four consecutive chapters repeat the same beat: the pursuers nearly
  catch the protagonist, she escapes by luck, the stakes reset.
- **Diagnosis:** the sequence lacks escalation (try/fail cycle); each failure costs
  nothing, so tension is flat. This is the sagging middle.
- **Fix:** convert the four beats into three rising attempts. Attempt 1 fails and costs
  her cover; attempt 2 fails and costs her ally; attempt 3 forces her to choose between
  the goal and the ally. Cut to three chapters and make each cost permanent. Example: end
  Ch. 10 not with escape but with the ally being taken.
- **Severity:** major.

### Example 2: prose (prose and voice, moderate)

- **Location:** Ch. 2, paragraph 4: "She saw the door was open and felt a chill, and she
  noticed the lights were off."
- **Observation:** three filter words ("saw," "felt," "noticed") mediate the perception,
  and the emotion is labeled rather than caused.
- **Diagnosis:** filtering keeps the reader at a distance and violates close point of
  view; "a chill" names the feeling instead of evoking it.
- **Fix:** delete the filters and render directly, then replace the emotion with a
  physical particular. Example: "The door stood open. The lights were off. Cold air moved
  from the room and touched the sweat on her neck."
- **Severity:** moderate.

### Example 3: character (character and arc, blocker)

- **Location:** Ch. 18 climax.
- **Observation:** the antagonist is defeated when a supporting character arrives and
  disarms him; the protagonist watches.
- **Diagnosis:** the protagonist does not cause the climax, so the arc's change is never
  tested; the reader's investment in growth goes unpaid.
- **Fix:** return the decisive action to the protagonist and make it require the truth she
  has resisted. Example: have her ally create the opening, but require the protagonist to
  choose between the lie she has clung to and the cost of acting. Rewrite the defeat as
  her choice, not the ally's arrival.
- **Severity:** blocker.

## Prioritized action plan

End every report with an ordered plan. Sort by severity, then by impact per unit of
effort. Cap it at roughly ten items so it is usable.

| # | Item | Category | Severity | Effort | Impact | Suggested order |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Return climax agency to protagonist | Character | Blocker | High | High | First: it fixes the arc |
| 2 | Compress the sagging middle | Structure | Major | Medium | High | Second: unlocks pacing |

Then give a short "if you only fix three things" shortlist for authors who want the
highest-leverage changes.

## Tone

Be direct and specific. Name what works as precisely as what fails. Avoid hedging
("maybe," "sort of"), avoid sarcasm, and avoid blanket praise. The author should be able
to act on every paragraph.
