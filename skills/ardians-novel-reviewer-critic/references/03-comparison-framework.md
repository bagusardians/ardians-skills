# Comparison Framework

How to place a work against other books. Comparison is not decoration; it tells the
author where the book sits in its field and what its readers expect. Every comparison must
be honest and evidence-based.

## Rules

- Compare on axes, not vague vibes. "Like Tolkien" is useless; "shares Tolkien's
  invented-language depth but not his mythic scope" is useful.
- Use three tiers: **canonical** (defining works), **recent comparables** (market peers,
  roughly the last 5-10 years), and **adjacent** (other genres or media with the same
  appeal).
- Ground claims about named published works in verifiable facts (premise, structure,
  reception). Never fabricate a quote, a plot point, or a sales claim. If relying on
  memory, label the comparison "from memory, verify."
- Do not reproduce long passages of the compared works.
- A comparison must yield an action or a positioning insight, not just a name.

## The comp axes

Score the manuscript and each comp on the same axes so the comparison is structured. Use
the manuscript's category scores plus the axes below.

| Axis | Question |
|---|---|
| Premise | How similar is the core idea and its angle? |
| Tone | Comic, grim, lyrical, propulsive, cozy, bleak? |
| Voice | Whose prose does this resemble, and how? |
| Structure | Linear, framed, epistolary, mosaic, multi-POV? |
| Pace | Slow-burn, breakneck, episodic, steady? |
| Scope | Intimate, epic, single-room, world-spanning? |
| Audience | Age, reading level, genre-reader expectations? |
| Theme | What is the book arguing, and does a comp argue the same? |
| Length | Comparable word count and act proportion? |

## Comp selection

1. Pick 3-5 comps total: at least one canonical, two recent market comps, and one
   adjacent.
2. For each, state the shared axis and the divergent axis, in one or two sentences.
3. State the reader takeaway: who should read this and why, and who should not.
4. Use the "readers who liked X will like this because Y" formula for each market comp.
5. If the manuscript resembles nothing in the current market, say so; that is a
   positioning fact, and note whether it is an advantage or a risk.

## Positioning statement

End the comparison section with a one-paragraph positioning statement:

> [Title] is a [genre] for readers of [comp A] and [comp B], set apart by [distinctive
> angle]. It will satisfy readers who want [promise], and it may disappoint readers
> expecting [expectation it does not meet].

## Comparison verdict

Give the work a standing in its field. Choose one and justify it with the axes.

- **Ahead of the field:** executes its genre better than current peers on most axes.
- **On par with the field:** meets genre expectations at a professional level.
- **Behind the field:** underperforms peers on core axes; name which.
- **Off-field:** pursues a different promise than its apparent genre; name the mismatch
  and whether it is deliberate and effective.

## Worked example

- **Manuscript:** a slow-burn secondary-world fantasy about a mapmaker who discovers the
  maps are rewriting the territory.
- **Canonical comp:** Tolkien's legendarium - shared: invented-world depth and
  cartographic obsession; divergent: intimate scope and single POV instead of mythic
  sweep.
- **Recent comp 1:** a contemporary quiet-fantasy about craft and obsession - shared:
  interiority and slow pace; divergent: lower stakes and no world-altering mechanism.
- **Recent comp 2:** a puzzle-driven fantasy about documents that alter reality - shared:
  premise engine; divergent: this manuscript is more character-led and less plot-led.
- **Adjacent:** literary fiction about obsession and unreliable perception - shared:
  theme and interiority; divergent: fantasy mechanism supplies external plot.
- **Reader takeaway:** "Readers who liked [quiet craft fantasy] will like the intimacy here;
  readers expecting [puzzle fantasy]'s relentless twists may find the pace slow."
- **Standing:** on par with the field on prose and character; behind the field on middle
  pacing.
